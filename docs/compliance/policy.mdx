<Hero eyebrow="Policy" title="The reason policy baselines stay in audit mode forever">
Someone turns on a recommended baseline, it blocks a controller that legitimately needs
what it blocks, a deploy fails at the worst moment, and the whole thing goes back to
warn-only. The baseline was right; it just did not know what was in the cluster.
</Hero>

## Policies are planned, not pasted

Admission policy in Stacktic is expressed as sub-components of a policy component —
resource constraints, pod-level security, image provenance, image signing, label
restrictions, enforcement of network policy and RBAC.

A plan is generated for **your** topology: which policies to enable at which profile, and
which namespaces each policy must exclude. The exclusions are not guesswork — they come
from the knowledge layer, keyed by component type, and are resolved against the components
your stack actually has.

<Steps>
  <Step title="Read the stack">
    Components, types and namespaces from the graph.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Choose the policy set">
    A named profile decides which policies are in scope.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Resolve carve-outs per policy">
    Operator-managed namespaces, components whose workloads cannot satisfy a given control,
    and anything you have declared as an exception.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Emit it as graph changes">
    The plan becomes sub-components and attributes. It is reviewed, applied and versioned
    like anything else — not applied directly to the cluster.
  </Step>
</Steps>

## The scan that makes it safe to enforce

Counting violations tells you whether a policy is doing something. It does not tell you
whether it is doing the *right* thing.

The deep admission scan performs a dry-run admission test per constraint, with a resource
that should be admitted and one that should not, and classifies the result:

| Classification | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Enforcing** | Admits what it should, rejects what it should not |
| **Not enforcing** | Present, but permitting what it is supposed to block |
| **Over-enforcing** | Rejecting things it should admit — a future outage |
| **Broken** | The constraint does not evaluate at all |

Over-enforcement is the finding nobody else reports, and it is the one that decides whether
you can move a baseline from audit to enforce.

## Coverage is part of the score

The scan also reports namespace coverage: which namespaces a constraint actually applies
to, and which ones fall outside every policy. A cluster where the policies are excellent
and the important namespace is excluded scores accordingly.

## Live state

Beyond planning and testing, the constraint inventory, the templates behind them, the live
violations per constraint and an overall compliance summary are all readable — by a person,
by the dashboard, or by an AI client answering "are we enforcing what we think we are".
